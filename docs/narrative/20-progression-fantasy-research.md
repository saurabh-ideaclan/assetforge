# 20. Progression fantasy research: hunter and awakening stories

Purpose: give the creative director a working map of the Korean hunter / awakening genre, so an original interactive visual novel can use its mechanisms without copying any single work. Solo Leveling is used only as the genre's best-known reference.

**Evidence note.** Web search (standard mode, 8 queries) returned mostly fan blogs, community threads and vendor pages. Hard facts are cited; craft claims marked **[unverified]** are the author's synthesis from genre familiarity and could not be confirmed from a reliable source. Treat all numeric spacing and length figures as starting hypotheses to test in playtests, not industry rules.

---

## 1. Storytelling mechanisms of the genre

The genre is a modern-day city with a supernatural economy bolted on. Each mechanism below turns a feeling readers already have (money worries, rank anxiety, wanting to be seen) into plot machinery.

| Mechanism | What it does | Why it engages |
|---|---|---|
| **Gates / dungeons as an economy** | Portals produce danger and also resources (materials, income, land value). Hunting is a job, with insurance, contracts and clean-up crews. | Stakes are concrete: a failed raid is rent unpaid, not just a death. It makes the fantasy feel like a labour market the reader recognises. |
| **Ranks as a caste** | A public letter grade, set at awakening, decides pay, respect and who gets protected. | Readers instantly know who matters. A rank is a visible score that the hero can later contradict. |
| **Guild power** | Guilds act as employers, militias and political blocs. | Gives antagonists with institutional weight, and gives loyalty a price. |
| **Awakening as social mobility** | One event, early in life, sorts people. It works like an exam result that cannot be retaken. | Mirrors real fears about fixed status. "Can I change my category?" is the genre's central hope. |
| **The weakest-hunter frame** | The lead starts at or near the bottom, dependent on others, often supporting family. | Sympathy first, power later. The distance to travel is built in. |
| **Visible leveling / a status view** | Progress is shown as numbers, tasks or notifications. | Satisfies the same loop as games: clear goal, feedback, reward. Reviewers of Solo Leveling highlight the pleasure of watching the hero level up ([Wikipedia summary of reception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solo_Leveling)). |
| **Second awakening / class change** | A later event that overturns the first sorting. | The turning point that lets the story reopen a "settled" hierarchy without denying that hierarchy existed. |
| **Regression and second-chance variants** | The lead returns to an earlier point with knowledge of what is coming. | A commentator on Korean web fiction notes that regression, possession and reincarnation are common openers because the premise works within a few episodes ([youngju.dev](https://www.youngju.dev/blog/storytelling/web-novel-grammar.en); personal blog, low reliability). Ranking sites also note that terms like "second chance" and "regression" are used loosely and interchangeably ([noveltranslator rankings](https://noveltranslator.com/rankings/top-11-korean-second-chance-novels); low reliability). |

Context: Solo Leveling began as a KakaoPage web novel in 2016 and became a webtoon in 2018 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solo_Leveling)). Progression fantasy in general is defined by power accumulation being a primary driver of the narrative, not a subplot ([Wikipedia: Progression fantasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progression_fantasy)).

**Why it works.** Three promises at once: the world is unfair in a way you recognise, the unfairness is measurable, and the measure can be beaten by effort plus one hidden advantage. It is a fantasy of overdue recognition, not of luck.

Reviewers criticised some adaptations as heavy on exposition and mechanics ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solo_Leveling)). The cheap version of the genre is a stat sheet with a revenge list.

## 2. Reader-satisfaction beats

Common rule behind all seven: **the payoff is felt in proportion to how clearly the reader saw the cost, the doubt and the audience beforehand.** Spacing figures are **[unverified]** craft estimates for a roughly 12 to 20 chapter season.

### 2.1 The underestimated character proves ability in public
- **Setup:** A named audience with a stated opinion (a rank, a joke, a policy). The reader must have heard the underestimate said aloud more than once.
- **Failures:** Faceless or cartoonishly stupid doubters; proof with no witness who changes their mind.
- **Spacing:** A small version every 3 to 5 chapters, one large one near the midpoint and one at the climax.

### 2.2 Surviving extraordinary danger while weak
- **Setup:** The reader knows the hero's actual limits (what they cannot do, what they carry, who depends on them).
- **Failures:** Survival by luck repeated; the danger never stays dangerous afterwards; the hero is saved by a rule the reader did not know.
- **Spacing:** Early and often, because it is the genre's main source of suspense in the "weak" phase. 

### 2.3 Recognition after long hardship
- **Setup:** Hardship that is specific and repeated (a bill, a closed door, a face that looks away).
- **Failures:** Recognition arrives before the hardship has cost anything; recognition comes from people the reader never met; it fixes everything at once.
- **Spacing:** Partial recognition early (one person), institutional recognition late. Never grant both in one chapter.

### 2.4 Strategic use of an underestimated ability
- **Setup:** The ability must be shown as weak or silly first, with its rules stated plainly in simple language.
- **Failures:** Rules change mid-fight; the ability is secretly just "strong"; the trick needs hidden information.
- **Spacing:** One fair-play puzzle per arc. Readers should be able to guess the trick one beat before the hero acts.

### 2.5 Sudden reversal of a power dynamic
- **Setup:** A hierarchy the reader has been made to resent, shown in at least two scenes.
- **Failures:** The villain was never shown doing harm; the hero then bullies the weak; too frequent, so dominance becomes the tone.
- **Spacing:** Rare.

### 2.6 Discovering hidden capability
- **Setup:** A planted clue of difference before the reveal.
- **Failures:** It solves the main problem, has no downside, or arrives by lecture.
- **Spacing:** The first discovery within the first act, but it should create a new problem (secrecy, debt, suspicion). Each further discovery should be smaller than the previous or come with a price.

### 2.7 Victories with personal cost
- **Setup:** Something the reader has watched the hero protect: a person, a promise, a safe routine.
- **Failures:** Cost is told, never seen again; or always falls on a disposable side character.
- **Spacing:** Roughly every second major victory. A story where every win is free reads as shallow; a story where every win hurts reads as punishment.

## 3. Pacing conventions

What could be verified: serialization is per-episode and each episode has to hold readers on its own, since authors are often paid per episode ([Korea JoongAng Daily](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/korea/short-addictive-web-novels-hook-more-readers-anyone-can-write-them-and-biggest-hits-have-become-dramas-musicals/10152441)). A Korean study of Munpia works links frequent, uninterrupted serialization to continued reading ([Kyobo Scholar abstract](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010027982764)). Vertical-scroll webtoon episodes are commonly cited at roughly 20 to 50 panels, with the final panels reserved for a hook, but that figure comes from vendor guidance and should not be relied on ([LlamaGen guide](https://llamagen.ai/blogs/how-many-panels-in-a-webtoon-episode); low reliability). I could not verify exact word counts per episode on KakaoPage or Munpia.

Everything below is synthesis, **[unverified]**:

- **Chapter length:** one sitting of about 3 to 6 minutes, roughly 40 to 90 text boxes plus one or two choices.
- **Hook cadence:** each chapter ends on an open question, reversal or new threat, with a smaller hook mid-chapter.
- **First awakening:** very early, in the opening chapter or the first few, because the genre's premise is the sorting itself. The story is about what the awakening *means*, so it may be shown, failed or already in the past.
- **Misery before hope:** a short misery section (about one to three chapters of real pressure) then a first glimmer. The glimmer should be a *problem or a door*, not a win. Long unbroken misery loses readers on mobile.
- **Power-scaling discipline:** keep a ladder with named rungs and one clear cost per rung. Community advice repeats this: set a scale and stay consistent, make some enemies resistant to the hero's strengths, and use scarce resources to make each upgrade matter ([r/ProgressionFantasy thread on the "treadmill"](https://lr.sudovanilla.org/r/ProgressionFantasy/comments/ff1i15/the_progression_treadmill_thoughts_on_a_potential/i3rqqdr/?context=3); opinion, not doctrine).
- **Avoiding an overpowered lead too early:** keep three dials separate: raw strength, knowledge, standing. The hero can rise on one while falling on another. Replace "bigger enemy" with a *different kind* of problem (social, legal, moral). The same community thread warns that when stronger enemies simply replace weaker ones, the sense of progress fades.
- **Ending discipline:** a visual novel can be finite, which avoids the endless treadmill.

## 4. Family drama and conspiracy

Evidence here is thin; this section is mostly pattern description **[unverified]**.

- **Family as the reason, not the prize.** Hunting is often undertaken to pay for a parent's care or a sibling's school. That ties private stakes to the economy of gates. The danger is when the family exists only to be rescued.
- **Keep the world bigger than the lead.** Conspiracies (a guild, ministry or company) have their own momentum and other victims. The hero changes *where it breaks*, not whether it exists.
- **Parents with moral complexity (patterns only).**
  - The absent parent who left to protect the family, and whose protection looks like abandonment.
  - The parent bound to an institution, obeying orders they privately doubt.
  - The parent who made a bad compromise (a debt, a silence, a deal) for a good reason.
  - The protective parent whose care controls the child's choices.
  Good versions let the reader sympathise and disagree in the same chapter.
- **Relatives are inside the caste system too,** so family conflict has a structural cause beyond personality.

## 5. Interactive adaptation

What could be verified:

- Quality-based narrative, as in Fallen London, makes story chunks appear based on changeable state, which Failbetter describes as the core idea ([Failbetter diary](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/storynexus-developer-diary-1-worlds-exist)).
- Emily Short notes that such systems are explicit about requirements and odds, so players choose when to risk and bad outcomes feel fair; she also notes that showing why an option is locked helps planning, and that some players find it too game-like ([Emily Short, high-agency narrative systems](https://emshort.blog/2017/05/25/mailbag-high-agency-narrative-systems/)).
- Branch-and-bottleneck structure reconverges paths later; guides advise tracking state so variation can persist without multiplying branches, and warn that choices that converge immediately feel illusory ([IF craft corpus on branching](https://pvliesdonk.github.io/if-craft-corpus/corpus-docs/narrative-structure/branching_narrative_construction); practitioner opinion).

I could not find reliable, citable design documentation for Choices, Episode or Disco Elysium; statements about them below are **[unverified]** and based on general knowledge.

**Progression stats.**
- Use four to six story-named stats, not one "power" number.
- Separate *capability* (what you can do), *standing* (how institutions see you) and *relationships* (what individuals owe you). Progression fantasy needs all three to move at different speeds.

**Reveal versus conceal.**
- Show costs up front when the player is *planning*; conceal outcomes when the player is *reacting*. A quiet note ("they will remember this") signals that choices register.
- Faction trust works as two values, trust and knowledge, that can diverge.

**Branch and converge with persistent consequences.**
- Converge on *scenes*, not on *states*. Everyone reaches the same gate raid, but who is beside you, what you carry and what was said differs.
- Spend early choices on state, later choices on structure.
- Callbacks matter more than new branches: one choice from chapter 2 should change a line in chapter 9.

**What makes a choice feel meaningful here.**
1. Each option costs something different (time, trust, safety, secrecy).
2. The player can predict the *kind* of consequence but not the full result.
3. The consequence returns, in a visible way, within a few chapters.
4. No option is a pure "good" or "evil" label.
5. The progression system itself offers choices (what to build, what to hide), not just rewards.

## 6. Red lines: distinctive Solo Leveling elements not to copy

The director should treat all of the following as off-limits, both directly and in thin disguise:

- The specific interface: a private daily-quest panel, penalty zones, a "player" being told what to do by a hidden system, and the exact stat-sheet presentation and notification style.
- The army of summoned former enemies serving the hero, including the extraction/summoning mechanic and the named loyal servants.
- Its specific lore terms, class titles, rank labels as used there, and named monsters, bosses and gods.
- Named organizations and the specific national hunter association arrangement and guild names.
- Signature scenes and sequences: the double-gate trap that starts the hero's change, the hidden-class test reveal, the repeated-trial instant dungeon, the staged public power reveals.
- The protagonist's exact starting package (lowest rank, hospital-bound parent, supporting a sibling), its endgame structure, and its shadow-themed visual signature.

Generic blocks (gates, ranks, awakening, leveling, guilds, regression) are genre commons, but rename and reshape them. **[Unverified: the list reflects well-known features only; no reference work was reviewed in detail. Check before final naming.]**

## 7. Design principles

1. **The first awakening should create a problem, not solve one.** Make it expensive, risky or dangerous to reveal.
2. **Show the caste before the hero climbs it.** The reader must feel the ranking in at least three everyday scenes (pay, transport, school or work, queue).
3. **Give every rank a visible cost of living.** If rank has no consequence outside combat, the caste is decoration.
4. **Make the weak phase about decisions.** Survival in early chapters should come from a choice the player made.
5. **Plant every strength as a weakness first.** The underestimated ability must be mocked, explained simply, then used cleverly.
6. **Keep three dials separate: strength, knowledge, standing.** Never raise all three in one chapter.
7. **Price every power gain.** Each rung brings a limit, a debt, a secret or an obligation the reader has seen.
8. **Ration public humiliation reversals.** Two or three per season, and at least one should cost the hero.
9. **Recognition arrives in layers.** First one person, then a group, then an institution.
10. **Give the world its own clock.** Conspiracy, guilds and gates move without the player.
11. **Parents are people with a past.** Each parent gets a reasonable motive, a harmful action and an unresolved question.
12. **Do not let the family exist to be saved.** At least one relative should act on their own goals and surprise the lead.
13. **Add a second kind of problem when power rises.** Replace "stronger monster" with legal, moral, social or secrecy pressure.
14. **Use few, named stats that gate dialogue and options.** Show why a locked option is locked, once, to teach the system.
15. **Show cost before choice when the player is planning, hide it when reacting.** Keep a consistent rule.
16. **Converge scenes, not outcomes.** Who stands beside you changes what happens in shared set pieces.
17. **Every major choice returns within three chapters,** by a line, a face or a closed door.
18. **Track trust and knowledge separately for each faction.** A faction that trusts you but knows little behaves differently from one that knows much and trusts little.
19. **End each chapter on a question or a reversal, and vary the type.** Never repeat the same hook shape three times running.
20. **Write for a phone in simple English:** short sentences, one idea per box, concrete nouns. Land a real ending instead of a treadmill.
